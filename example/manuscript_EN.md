# RICTOR gates a methionine-cycle bifurcation into two longevity arms: a hypothesis of SAM-dependent methylation reprogramming and succinate-dependent mitophagy

## Abstract

Dietary methionine restriction has extended lifespan in rodents since the 1990s, yet the metabolic route by which methionine feeds longevity remains incompletely mapped. RICTOR, the defining subunit of mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), is an established regulator of longevity, but the specific outputs through which it acts on the methionine cycle are only partly characterized. A recent study demonstrated that RICTOR restrains longevity and osmotic tolerance in Caenorhabditis elegans through a methionine-cycle–mitophagy axis that requires two vitamin B12-dependent enzymes: METR-1 (methionine synthase, one-carbon cycle) and MMCM-1 (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, mitochondrial propionate catabolism). That work traced the MMCM-1 arm—propionate-to-succinate flux driving mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy—but left the METR-1 arm, which feeds the methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), mechanistically unexamined. I hypothesize that RICTOR sits at a methionine-cycle bifurcation that emits two coordinated longevity signals: a succinate arm driving mitophagy, and a SAM arm driving global methylation reprogramming of DNA, histone H3K4, and RNA m6A. I propose that the SAM/methylation arm is an independent—rather than merely redundant—contributor to lifespan, and I derive three falsifiable predictions, each testable in the rict-1 system with existing loss-of-function and pharmacological tools. If correct, the methionine cycle would emerge as a single metabolic node whose two outputs jointly couple dietary methionine and vitamin B12 to organismal ageing.

**Keywords**: RICTOR; mTORC2; methionine cycle; S-adenosylmethionine; mitophagy; longevity

## 1. Introduction

RICTOR is the defining subunit of mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex 2, a rapamycin-insensitive arm of the conserved TOR kinase that was first identified as a RICTOR-bound complex distinct from the raptor-containing mTORC1 [1]. mTORC2 phosphorylates and activates AGC-family kinases—most canonically Akt on its hydrophobic motif Ser473—and its structural integrity depends on the obligatory subunit SIN1/MIP1 [2–4]. This places mTORC2 at the top of a signalling cascade that, together with mTORC1, integrates nutrient and growth-factor input to govern growth, metabolism and, in model organisms, ageing [5,6]. In Caenorhabditis elegans, the TOR pathway limits lifespan: loss of RICTOR/TORC2 promotes fat remodelling and extended life span [7], and genetic or pharmacological attenuation of TOR prolongs life through the transcription factors SKN-1/Nrf and the forkhead factor DAF-16/FoxO, the founding longevity transcription factor [8–12].

A second, largely parallel literature places methionine at the centre of the same ageing question. Dietary methionine restriction extends lifespan in rodents [13,14], and methionine sits at the hub of one-carbon metabolism, which converts methionine into the universal methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) [15–17]. The one-carbon balance is not merely a substrate supply but a regulatory signal: fluctuations in one-carbon metabolism are directly sensed and translated into genome-wide changes in histone methylation [18], and methionine availability dictates the pluripotency-versus-differentiation state of human stem cells in an epigenetic manner [19]. SAM, in turn, is the obligatory methyl donor for DNA methylation by the DNMT family [20], for trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3), which regulates lifespan in the C. elegans germline [21,22], and for RNA m6A methylation [23].

A third body of work establishes that mitochondrial quality control, and specifically mitophagy, is a core determinant of longevity: PINK1 recruits the E3 ligase Parkin to depolarised mitochondria to tag them for autophagic clearance [24,25], and mitophagy declines with age in C. elegans, where its restoration extends lifespan [26]. The tricarboxylic-acid (TCA)-cycle intermediate succinate is meanwhile recognised not only as a metabolic hub but as a signalling metabolite with broad cellular consequences, from inflammatory gene induction to mitochondrial reactive-oxygen signalling [27,28].

These three themes converge in a recent study by Motwani and colleagues, which showed that RICTOR restrains an interspecies host–microbe dialogue that influences longevity through a methionine-cycle–mitophagy axis [29]. In C. elegans, loss of rict-1/RICTOR, on a vitamin B12-rich bacterial diet with optimal methionine, enhances osmotic tolerance and lifespan; strikingly, both phenotypes require two vitamin B12-dependent enzymes, the methionine synthase METR-1 (one-carbon cycle) and the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase MMCM-1 (mitochondrial propionate catabolism). That work dissected only one of the two outputs: succinate produced through MMCM-1 accumulates, fragments mitochondria, and activates mitophagy, which is required for the longevity [29]. The other output—methionine regenerated by METR-1 and its downstream methyl donor SAM—was shown to be necessary but was not mechanistically traced.

Here I propose that this second, unexamined arm acts through SAM-dependent methylation reprogramming, and that RICTOR therefore gates a single methionine-cycle bifurcation with two coordinated longevity signals: succinate–mitophagy and SAM–methylation. Consistent with the observation that a microbe-derived methionine/folate circuit already modulates C. elegans ageing [30], I argue the methionine cycle is a metabolic node whose two outputs jointly couple diet to lifespan.

## 2. Hypothesis

I hypothesize that RICTOR gates a methionine-cycle bifurcation (Figure 1) whose two products—SAM and succinate—constitute two distinct, coordinated arms of longevity, and that the SAM arm acts specifically through global methylation reprogramming of DNA, histone H3K4, and RNA m6A. Three propositions follow.

**Proposition 1 (a single bifurcation node).** The methionine cycle is not a linear pathway but a junction with two diverging, chemically independent outputs. Methionine synthase (METR-1) closes the one-carbon cycle to regenerate methionine, the substrate of SAM [15,17]; methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MMCM-1) converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, feeding succinate into the TCA cycle [29]. Both enzymes are vitamin B12-dependent, and in the rict-1 longevity context both are individually required [29]. RICTOR, as a conserved brake on this axis, therefore sits upstream of—and restrains—both arms simultaneously, rather than only one.

**Proposition 2 (the SAM arm acts through methylation).** The longevity-relevant consequence of the METR-1/SAM arm is not SAM abundance per se but the methylation it fuels. SAM is the obligatory methyl donor for DNMT-mediated DNA methylation [20], for H3K4 trimethylation by the COMPASS-family complex that regulates C. elegans lifespan [21,22], and for m6A deposition on mRNA catalysed by the METTL3–METTL14 methyltransferase complex [23,31]. The one-carbon-to-methylation link is mechanistically established: histone methylation dynamics are tuned by sensing one-carbon flux through SAM [18]. A recent study provides a direct molecular precedent for TOR-pathway signalling driving exactly this conversion: activation of the PI3K–AKT–mTOR axis upregulates phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) and serine metabolism, which in turn fuels SAM-dependent H3K4me3 at the NFATC2 locus to enforce CD8+ T cell exhaustion [32]. I propose that RICTOR loss, by de-repressing the methionine cycle, raises SAM and thereby drives a coordinated increase in DNA (DNMT1-dependent [33]), histone (H3K4me3 [21,22]), and RNA (m6A [23,34]) methylation, and that this reprogramming is itself a longevity signal.

**Proposition 3 (two coordinated, independently required arms).** The succinate–mitophagy arm and the SAM–methylation arm are parallel legs of a single metabolic switch rather than redundant readouts of one pathway. Because they originate from different enzymes (MMCM-1 versus METR-1) and different products (succinate versus SAM), they can in principle be decoupled; because both are required for the rict-1 phenotype [29], I propose they converge on longevity through distinct processes—mitochondrial quality control versus transcriptional/epigenetic reprogramming—and that their joint, rather than additive, action explains the robustness of the phenotype.

The central claim is that the SAM arm's longevity-relevant output is methylation. This is a specific, refutable commitment: SAM also feeds polyamine synthesis and transsulfuration, and if the rict-1 lifespan extension survives global inhibition of methylation, then the SAM arm does not act through methylation and the hypothesis is wrong (see Section 4).

## 3. Evaluation of the hypothesis

**What is directly evidenced versus inferred.** Several links rest directly on published data: RICTOR restrains the methionine-cycle–mitophagy axis and requires both METR-1 and MMCM-1 [29]; the succinate arm drives fragmentation and mitophagy required for longevity [29]; methionine restriction prolongs life [13,14]; SAM is the universal methyl donor [17]; one-carbon flux tunes histone methylation [18]; SAM-dependent H3K4me3 regulates lifespan [21,22]; and TOR-pathway signalling can drive SAM-dependent H3K4me3 through serine/one-carbon metabolism [32]. By contrast, the step I introduce—that the METR-1/SAM arm of the RICTOR axis acts through global methylation reprogramming, and does so as an independent longevity signal—is proposed, not yet measured. This distinguishes the hypothesis from a mere restatement of Motwani et al.: the bifurcation and the dual requirement are already established there; the novel, testable content is the methylation identity of the SAM arm and its independence from mitophagy.

**Grading the supporting evidence.** I rank the load-bearing links by strength. Strong (directly measured in the source system): RICTOR restrains the methionine-cycle–mitophagy axis and requires both METR-1 and MMCM-1 [29]; the succinate arm drives mitophagy required for longevity [29]; methionine restriction extends lifespan across rodents [13,14]. Moderate (established in adjacent systems, assumed transferable): SAM is the universal methyl donor [17]; one-carbon flux tunes histone methylation [18]; SAM-dependent H3K4me3 regulates lifespan in the C. elegans germline [21,22]. Weakest (the proposed step): that the METR-1/SAM arm of the RICTOR axis acts through global methylation and constitutes an independent longevity signal. The hypothesis therefore does not rest on its weakest link; it restates strong, published findings and adds one clearly flagged, directly testable inference.

**A single-system advantage.** Unlike hypotheses that splice together two independent contexts, my two arms are empirical products of a single paper and a single organism: METR-1 and MMCM-1 were shown together, in the same rict-1 mutant, to be jointly required [29]. The bifurcation is therefore an observed fact of the system, not an inference I impose across unrelated datasets.

**Relationship to prior work.** The link between methionine/SAM and lifespan is a crowded field, and the idea that mTOR modulation perturbs histone methylation has precedent: modulation of mTOR induces selective perturbations in histone methylation that influence cell phenotypes [35]. That work, however, concerns the canonical mTORC1 pathway and treats methylation as a downstream effector of mTOR inhibition generally; it does not identify RICTOR/mTORC2 as the gate, does not place the methionine cycle at a bifurcation between SAM and succinate, and does not posit mitophagy as the co-ordinate arm. More broadly, existing methylation-centric accounts of mTOR action treat histone methylation as one of many outputs of altered nucleotide/one-carbon flux, without assigning it a specific, enzyme-defined metabolic source or a specific longevity-relevant readout. To my knowledge, no study has yet framed RICTOR specifically as gating a methionine-cycle bifurcation whose two products—SAM-dependent methylation and succinate-dependent mitophagy—act as coordinated longevity arms, nor proposed that the SAM arm acts through methylation as an independent requirement in the rict-1 system. I state this as a hedged priority claim pending a full citation survey.

**The honest boundary: SAM has multiple fates.** My key inference is the most open to challenge, and I flag it explicitly. SAM is a precursor not only to methylation but to polyamines (via decarboxylated SAM) and, through homocysteine, to transsulfuration/cysteine [15,17,36]. It is therefore possible that the METR-1 requirement in rict-1 longevity reflects a non-methylation SAM fate. The predictions below are designed precisely to adjudicate this: they use methylation-specific inhibitors and methylation readouts, so that a survival of the phenotype under methylation blockade would refute Proposition 2 without invalidating the bifurcation framing itself.

**Limitations.** (1) The methylation identity of the SAM arm is inferred, not measured. (2) The two arms may be coupled through shared upstream flux rather than through an RICTOR-specific allocation mechanism; the term "gate" is a functional description, not a claim of direct molecular action. (3) Each assumption about enzyme identities leans on the single study [29] and should be independently confirmed. (4) Global methylation is heterogeneous across loci and cell types; a bulk increase need not translate to the specific transcriptional programme relevant to longevity. (5) The proposal is by a single author and rests primarily on a comparative reading of the literature rather than new experimental data; it is offered for experimental adjudication, not as a review.

## 4. Hypothesis testing

Each prediction is framed as a class of experiment achievable in the rict-1 C. elegans system with existing loss-of-function and pharmacological tools; as is appropriate for a hypothesis article, only categories of method are indicated.

**P1 — the SAM arm acts through methylation (necessity).** Treat long-lived rict-1 mutant animals with inhibitors of DNA methyltransferase (for example 5-aza compounds [20]), genetic or pharmacological ablation of the H3K4 methylase complex (SET-2/ASH-2/COMPASS in C. elegans [21]), or inactivation of the m6A writer METTL3/METTL14 [23,31]. If the lifespan extension is partially or wholly reversed, then SAM-dependent methylation contributes to longevity. The prediction is refuted if rict-1 animals remain long-lived despite demonstrable blockade of methylation—measured as reduced global DNA methylation, H3K4me3, and m6A—which would indicate the SAM arm acts through a non-methylation fate (polyamines or transsulfuration [36]).

**P2 — the two arms are independent (non-redundancy).** Combine (a) a methylation-side intervention (as in P1) with (b) inhibition of mitophagy (for example loss of the mitophagy receptor DCT-1/PDR-1 [25,26]) or of MMCM-1/succinate flux in the rict-1 background. If double intervention suppresses lifespan more than either intervention alone—tested by a full two-way interaction—the two arms are independent and coordinated rather than redundant. The prediction is refuted if single blockade of one arm fully recapitulates the double blockade; this would collapse the model to a single essential arm.

**P3 — a molecular readout with arm selectivity (specificity).** Measure global DNA methylation, H3K4me3, and m6A levels in wild-type versus rict-1 animals, in each case comparing animals that lack METR-1 versus MMCM-1. The prediction is that rict-1 animals show elevated methylation marks relative to wild type, and that this elevation is dependent on METR-1 but not on MMCM-1, mirroring the enzyme-specific requirement reported for the longevity phenotype [29]. The prediction is refuted if the methylation marks are elevated in a manner independent of METR-1, or equally dependent on MMCM-1, which would sever the proposed mapping of SAM versus succinate to methylome versus mitophagy.

P3 is my decisive test: it ties each arm to a distinct molecular signature and a distinct upstream enzyme in a single experiment. A pattern in which METR-1 controls methylation but MMCM-1 controls mitophagy would confirm the bifurcation; a pattern in which both enzymes control both readouts would indicate a shared upstream flux rather than a two-arm bifurcation.

**Design controls shared across predictions.** Each experiment must guard against the possibility that a manipulation shortens or lengthens lifespan through general toxicity or metabolic perturbation rather than through the methylome. I therefore specify that every intervention be paired with (a) a direct readout of the targeted methylation mark or mitophagy flux, and (b) a survival curve scored with pre-registered criteria, adequate biological replicates, and appropriate statistics, so that a positive result is claimed only when the molecular readout moves in the predicted direction in parallel with the lifespan phenotype. The rict-1 system is chosen deliberately because the two candidate enzymes can be ablated independently in the same genetic background, isolating enzyme-specific effects more cleanly than pharmacological treatment alone.

## 5. Consequences of the hypothesis and discussion

If the hypothesis holds, the methionine cycle would resolve into a single metabolic node whose two outputs—mitophagy and methylation—jointly translate dietary methionine and vitamin B12 into lifespan, with RICTOR as the restraint on both. This would recast the well-known methionine-restriction effect not as a monotone "less methionine" readout but as a coordinated reduction in two arms, and would predict that interventions targeting either arm—mitophagy inducers or methylation modulators—might each capture a fraction of the longevity benefit.

The most direct mechanistic consequence is a testable molecular mapping: SAM → methylome, succinate → mitochondrial morphology. If confirmed, the methionine cycle would offer a two-axis readout—methylation state and mitophagy flux—that jointly reports the dietary/metabolic state that RICTOR restrains, and could serve as a composite biomarker or intervention surface for ageing.

A conceptual consequence arises even if the methylation identity is refuted: the bifurcation framing—RICTOR restraining two independently required, enzyme-specific arms—would survive intact and simply point to a different SAM fate (polyamines or transsulfuration) as the second arm. Conversely, if P2 shows the arms are redundant, the field would gain a parsimonious single-arm view of the axis, itself a useful consolidation.

Beyond model organisms, the hypothesis carries a testable mammalian corollary. mTORC2 signalling declines in ageing mammalian tissues, and genetic disruption of mTORC2 extends mouse lifespan in a manner uncoupled from its insulin-sensitising effects [10]. If the methionine-cycle bifurcation is conserved, the two-arm model predicts that RICTOR/mTORC2 loss reprograms both mitochondrial quality and the methylome in mammals, and that at least some of the heterogeneity across methionine-restriction and mTOR-inhibition longevity studies reflects the relative weighting of the two arms rather than a single dominant mechanism. This offers a framework for reconciling seemingly conflicting results on whether lifespan tracks methylome changes, mitophagy flux, or both.

Finally, I note the convergence with an adjacent line of evidence that a microbial folate/methionine circuit modulates C. elegans ageing [30]. Together these observations suggest the methionine cycle is a point at which host signalling (RICTOR/mTORC2) and microbial input (folate/vitamin B12) intersect to set lifespan. The hypothesis is deliberately scoped so that its falsification—whichever arm fails—still tightens that emerging picture.

In conclusion, I propose that RICTOR acts at a single methionine-cycle bifurcation emitting two coordinated longevity signals—SAM-dependent methylation reprogramming and succinate-dependent mitophagy—and that the methylation arm is an independent rather than redundant contributor to lifespan. Three falsifiable predictions, each testable in the rict-1 system with existing tools, are offered so that the hypothesis can be adjudicated; whichever arm survives testing, the methionine cycle is likely to emerge as the node at which diet, epigenetics, and mitochondrial quality jointly set the pace of ageing.
